# Retail Sales Analysis

## Problem
The company has records of a million rows. Each row has one product on one invoice: the invoice number, product code and description, quantity, date, unit price, customer ID and country. But it does not understand the records: it cannot tell which products and which customers bring in the money or when demand rises and falls, so it cannot take decisions about stock and marketing.
## Why it matters
- Money gets tied up in stock that sells slowly, while popular items run out.
- A few large wholesale customers may account for a big share of revenue, and losing one unnoticed is expensive.
- Without knowing the seasonal pattern, the company orders too late or too much.

## Questions
- Which products generate the most sales, and which sell poorly?
- How do sales change over the months, and which products are seasonal?
- Who are our most loyal and most valuable customers, and how much do they spend?
- Which countries outside the UK bring in the most revenue?
- Which customers used to buy regularly but have churned?

Each finding will come with a recommended action for the business, for example on inventory management.
## Findings

### 1. Top products by revenue
- A few products dominate: the Regency Cakestand 3 Tier (about £331k) earned over twice as much as the 4th-ranked product, and the top two products together brought in about £592k.
- Most top sellers sell all year round. Only two of the top 10 are strongly seasonal: the Paper Chain Kit 50's Christmas and the Rotating Silver Angels T-Light Holder, each earning over 70% of its revenue in September–November (the average product earns 36% in those months).

**Recommended action:** Keep the top year-round products (the Regency Cakestand, Cream Hanging Heart and Jumbo Bags) in stock at all times, since running out would cost the most revenue. Plan stock of the seasonal top sellers separately, around the autumn peak.
### 2. Sales over the year
- Sales follow the same pattern in both years: they are low from January to August, climb from September, and peak in November at about £1.5 million, around twice the level of the quietest months (February and April).
- The peak comes before Christmas because the customers are mainly shops, which buy their Christmas stock one to three months in advance.
- December 2011 is excluded from the comparison, because the data ends on 9 December 2011.
- The most seasonal products fall into two groups: Christmas items (advent calendars, decorations, festive tableware) and cold-weather items (hand warmers), each earning 85–94% of their revenue in September–November. Most were first sold in mid-September 2010, suggesting the company launches its seasonal range each September.

**Recommended action:** Have stock of the top and Christmas products in place by the end of August, ready for the September–November peak. Use the quiet months (January–April) for promotions or clearance.
### 3. Most valuable and most loyal customers
- Revenue is highly concentrated: the top 10 of 5,878 known customers (0.17%) bring in 16% of the revenue from known customers.
- Valuable and loyal are not the same. Only 4 customers are in both the top 10 by revenue and the top 10 by number of orders.
- There are two very different kinds of important customer: big occasional buyers (e.g. customer 12415: 28 orders, about £5,160 per order) and small frequent buyers (e.g. customer 12748: 336 orders, about £160 per order).
- This covers only sales with a Customer ID, about 85% of total revenue.

**Recommended action:** Give the top 10 customers personal account management: a named contact, priority stock and regular check-ins. Watch big occasional buyers closely, since one missed order from them costs thousands; finding 5 will look at customers who stopped buying.
### 4. Countries outside the UK
- The top markets outside the UK are Ireland (EIRE, £659k), the Netherlands (£554k), Germany (£425k) and France (£350k).
- These markets work very differently. Ireland's revenue comes from just 5 customers (about £132k each) and the Netherlands' from 22, while Germany (107 customers, about £4k each) and France (95) have many smaller customers.

**Recommended action:** In Ireland and the Netherlands, protect the few large accounts with personal account management, and look for new customers to reduce the dependence on them. In Germany and France, focus on growing order sizes and attracting larger wholesale buyers.
### 5. Customers who stopped buying
- A regular customer (at least 5 orders) is counted as churned when they have not ordered for more than twice their usual time between orders, and for more than 90 days.
- 374 of 2,161 regular customers (17%) have churned. Together they had spent £1.37 million, 8% of the revenue from known customers.
- Several were among the most frequent buyers. For example, customer 17850 placed 155 orders, about one every 2 days, and has not ordered since December 2010.
- None of the top 10 customers by revenue has churned. Of the top 10 by number of orders, one has: customer 17850.

**Recommended action:** Have the sales team contact the most valuable churned customers first, starting with the top 10 by past spending, to learn why they left and try to win them back. Before that, check whether any of them simply moved to a new account. Track this list monthly so lapsing customers are spotted early, before they are lost.
## Limitations

- The data has selling prices but not product costs, so we measure revenue, not profit.
- The data records sales only, not stock levels, so we cannot see when items ran out.
